# Arctic Fox (*Vulpes lagopus*, winter coat) — Quantitative Reference Notes

Compiled for measurement purposes only. No files were downloaded into the repo;
this document is the only artifact produced. Every number below is flagged:

- **[MEASURED]** — taken directly from a specimen, live animal, or calibrated
  instrument, reported in a source I read (primary) or that a search engine
  quoted verbatim from a source I could not fully open (secondary — noted).
- **[ESTIMATED]** — a value the source itself calls an estimate, average, or
  model output.
- **[DERIVED]** — arithmetic I did on someone else's measured numbers (e.g. a
  ratio from two measured lengths). The inputs are measured; the ratio is mine.
- **[INFERRED-FROM-PHOTOGRAPHS]** — no specimen measurement exists; the number
  comes from reading a photograph (this project's own reference photos, or
  published photos) by eye.
- **[GAP]** — I looked and did not find a real number. Do not treat the
  ART_DIRECTION.md figure next to it as confirmed just because it's plausible.

Where two sources disagree, both are given. Nothing is averaged.

I could not obtain full primary-text access to two of the most important
sources for this brief — the Mammalian Species account and Underwood &
Reynolds (1980) — despite several access attempts (see §7). Where I rely on
search-engine-quoted fragments of those sources rather than text I read
myself, it's marked **[secondary quote]**.

## 1. Winter-coat share of silhouette — the ~48%/60mm question

**Verdict: not independently confirmable or refutable from the literature. No
source reports "coat as a percentage of silhouette radius." That is a derived
framing, not a measurement zoologists report.** The zoological literature
reports fur depth in mm and body size separately; nobody publishes the ratio
the project needs. Below is the best real data to reconstruct it yourself,
plus why each attempt falls short of a clean confirmation.

**Fur depth, absolute, winter, arctic fox specifically:**
- Scholander et al. (1950c), *Biological Bulletin* 99:225–236 — the
  foundational body-insulation study. Their Fig. 1 (reproduced in Prestrud
  1991, which I read in full) plots winter fur thickness (mm, x-axis 0–90) for
  a range of arctic/tropical mammals. The arctic fox ("white fox") point
  clusters together with reindeer/caribou, Dall sheep, wolf and grizzly bear —
  the thicker-coated group — while red fox, rabbit, marten and beaver sit
  lower. **[INFERRED-FROM-graph-description, not a printed digit]** — I have
  the figure's layout via Prestrud's reproduction, not pixel-level axis
  values, so I can say arctic fox winter fur thickness is plausibly in a
  **~40–60mm** band by visual clustering, but I cannot cite an exact mm value
  from this figure with confidence. The original 1950c paper likely has the
  source table; I did not get primary access to it (see §7).
- Underwood (1971, unpublished PhD thesis) and Underwood & Reynolds (1980,
  *Int. J. Biometeorology* 24:39–48), as summarized by Prestrud (1991), which
  I read in full: seasonal change in fur depth is "nearly 200%." **This is
  ambiguous in the secondary text** — it could mean winter fur is ~2x summer
  (a 100% increase, "nearly 200% of summer") or ~3x summer (a "200% increase").
  Other secondary tertiary sources (Wikipedia, IFAW, ADF&G) independently say
  "winter coat is twice as thick/long as summer" — i.e., ~2x, 100% increase —
  which is the more commonly repeated figure. **[secondary quote, magnitude
  uncertain by a factor that matters]**.
- 70% of winter coat is fine underfur by volume/mass — repeated across
  multiple secondary sources, ultimately traceable to Underwood & Reynolds
  (1980). **[secondary quote]**.

**Body radius, skin-only, winter:**
- The one real skin-diameter data point I found: Prestrud (1991), Fig. 5 —
  cross-section of a trapped carcass, cut just anterior to the hip, "diameter
  is about 15cm [150mm] and the intestine is seen embedded in fat. The
  subcutaneous fat is about 3–4cm [30–40mm] thick." **[MEASURED, but an
  outlier]**: this was an explicitly "extremely fat" individual (caught 1986,
  fat content estimated >50% by Prestrud's own regression, versus a Nov–Dec
  population average of 22% and a range of 3–38% across 38 carcasses,
  Prestrud 1982 thesis as cited in Prestrud 1991). It is also measured at the
  hip, not mid-torso. Treat "~75mm radius, skin+fat, at the hip, of an obese
  individual" as a loose upper-bound sanity check, not a mid-torso figure for
  a typical animal. It does not confirm or refute a 60mm mid-torso figure —
  it's a different location on a non-representative animal.
- I looked for a fur-trade "girth" or standardized pelt-board measurement that
  might back into a live circumference (pelts are graded on stretched board
  size). I found only flat, laid-pelt length × width figures (e.g. "34–42in
  long × 7–8in across" for average pelts; up to 55–60in × 9–10in for large
  dense ones). I did the geometry (a flat pelt's width relates to roughly half
  the live circumference) and got numbers in the same neighborhood as the
  project's estimate, but pelt tanning/stretching (which deliberately
  elongates and narrows pelts for grading) makes this conversion unreliable
  enough that I'm not reporting the resulting figure — it would be exactly
  the kind of confident-looking number built on a shaky inference chain this
  project has been burned by before. **[GAP]**: no reliable direct
  measurement of live/fresh mid-torso circumference or diameter, skin-only,
  was found.

**Bottom line for the fur team:** the qualitative regional pattern your
48%/60mm estimate implies (deep coat on trunk, shallow on head, thin body
underneath) is directionally supported by peer-reviewed thermal-biology work
(§2 below confirms head has *less* fur than trunk in all seasons, which is
consistent with your skull-coat-thinner-than-flank-coat assumption). The
specific percentage and the specific 60mm are plausible and not contradicted
by anything found, but they are not verified by any source I could access.
Treat them as **[ESTIMATED — unverified, plausible]**, not as confirmed.

## 2. Coat depth by body region, in millimetres

**Headline finding: a full region-by-region table (muzzle/forehead/skull/
cheek/throat/ruff/shoulder/back/flank/belly/haunch/legs/paw/tail
base-mid-tip/ear-both-surfaces) in millimetres, for live winter arctic foxes,
does not appear to exist in literature I could access.** The one study that
almost certainly has exactly this table — Underwood & Reynolds (1980),
*International Journal of Biometeorology* 24:39–48, "Photoperiod and fur
lengths in the arctic fox" — is paywalled at Springer and I could not obtain
the full text (see §7). Everything below is what I could get instead.

### 2a. Qualitative regional pattern — [MEASURED, primary source read in full]

Prestrud (1991), summarizing Underwood (1971) and Underwood & Reynolds
(1980) directly:

> "Considerable site specific variation exists in depth of fur both when
> making comparisons for a given month and seasonal comparisons for a given
> site. In general, the sites commonly in contact with the snow surface both
> when walking or lying down (foot pads, the posterior medial aspects of the
> lower legs and the lateral part of the trunk) show a substantial increase
> of fur depth in winter. The dorsal and lateral parts of the trunk exposed
> when lying down also have thick fur and show a substantial seasonal
> increase in fur depth. Those parts of the fox not in contact with the snow
> surface or exposed to the environment (distal portion of legs, head and
> ventral part of the trunk) have less fur than the previously defined areas
> in all seasons and show only minor seasonal changes."

Read carefully, this gives you a real, sourced **ranking**, even without mm
values:
1. Deepest, most seasonal: foot pads, posterior-medial lower leg, lateral
   trunk (flank).
2. Also deep, seasonal: dorsal trunk (back), lateral trunk exposed when
   curled up.
3. Shallowest, least seasonal: **head**, distal legs, ventral trunk (belly).

This directly supports the project's existing assumption that skull/head coat
should be *shallower* than flank/back coat — that ordering is real and
sourced. It does **not** support a specific mm depth for any of them.

### 2b. The only actual mm numbers I could find, by region

Source: Ellen Carrlee (conservator, Alaska State Museum), "Alaska Fur ID
Project" (alaskafurid.wordpress.com), an applied museum/trapper-education
resource, not a peer-reviewed ecological study, but built from a real,
directly measured specimen (Alaska State Museum specimen "ASM #2") plus
cross-references to two real fur-identification references (Brown 1942;
Adorjan & Kolenosky 1969, *A Manual for the Identification of Hairs of
Selected Ontario Mammals* — a genuine, widely-cited forensic/wildlife hair-ID
manual) and one fur-trade grading reference ("Furskin" 2006).

| Region | Guard hair length | Underfur length | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower back | 20–35 mm | 18–22 mm | ASM #2, direct specimen **[MEASURED]** |
| Belly | 80–85 mm | 40–50 mm | ASM #2, direct specimen **[MEASURED]** |
| Tail (undifferentiated — not split by base/mid/tip) | 60–70 mm | 25–30 mm | ASM #2, direct specimen **[MEASURED]** |
| General winter guard hair | 70–80 mm | 20–30 mm | Brown (1942) **[secondary quote]** |
| General winter guard hair | 40–50 mm | — | Adorjan & Kolenosky (1969) **[secondary quote]** |
| General winter guard hair | 75–85 mm | 15–30 mm | "Furskin" (2006), trade reference **[secondary quote, lower confidence — commercial fur-grading source]** |

**Important caveat before you put these into a shell-fur length map:** this
table measures guard hair **length** the way a fur grader does — combed or
pulled straight along its own axis. That is a different quantity from the
perpendicular **loft/depth** of the resting coat that a shell-fur system
wants, because hair lies at an angle to the skin and overlaps its neighbours.
For a coat combed flat against the body, effective visual loft is generally
*less* than the straight-line hair length, sometimes considerably so on areas
where hair lies nearly flat. This is likely why the belly numbers above (long
guard hair, 80–85mm) don't contradict Prestrud's qualitative finding that the
belly is one of the *thinnest*-coated regions in insulation terms (§2a) — long
individual hairs lying flat can still add up to less standing depth than
shorter, denser, more erect hair on the back. **Do not build a "belly is the
deepest region" length map from the mm table above; the peer-reviewed
insulation-depth ranking in §2a should govern depth, and the mm table should
at most inform individual hair-strand length for the fur shader, with the
belly's long, low-angle hair used for the "hangs down and hides the leg"
behaviour ART_DIRECTION.md already specifies.**

## 3. Skull and face proportions

### 3a. Muzzle length vs. cranium length — [MEASURED, primary source read in full]

**Nanova, O. & Prôa, M. (2017). "Cranial features of mainland and Commander
Islands (Russia) Arctic foxes (*Vulpes lagopus*) reflect their diverging
foraging strategies." *Polar Research* 36:7.** Open access (CC-BY-NC),
DOI: 10.1080/17518369.2017.1310976. I read this paper in full.

Method: dry adult crania, digital calipers, measured to 1mm accuracy, housed
at the Zoological Museum of M.V. Lomonosov Moscow State University.
CBL = condylobasal length (full skull length); RL = absolute rostrum
(muzzle) length.

| Population | n | CBL (mm, mean±SD) | RL (mm, mean±SD) | RL/CBL | Gape angle (°) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mainland (Chukchi Peninsula) | 43 (22M/21F) | 121.30 ± 4.18 | 74.12 ± 1.97 | 0.611 | 50.72 ± 5.42 |
| Bering Island | 41 (21M/20F) | 128.68 ± 4.92 | 77.32 ± 3.44 | 0.601 | 57.12 ± 7.43 |
| Mednyi Island | 30 (18M/12F) | 127.65 ± 4.82 | 74.93 ± 3.81 | 0.587 | 60.70 ± 6.23 |

**[DERIVED]** — the paper doesn't report a separate braincase length, but
subtracting: braincase-equivalent = CBL − RL. For mainland foxes:
47.18mm. So **muzzle : braincase-equivalent length ≈ 1.57 : 1** (mainland);
1.51:1 (Bering); 1.42:1 (Mednyi). Muzzle is **~61% of total skull length**
(mainland).

Sexual dimorphism note (also measured in this paper): males have longer
crania and rostra than females in all three populations, but rostrum
*proportions* (RL/CBL) do not differ by sex — so this ratio should be
sex-independent for your model.

**This is a skull (bone) ratio, not a live-tissue ratio.** Do not treat
1.57:1 as confirming or contradicting ART_DIRECTION.md §4c's "1.4–1.6:1
taper" target — that section is describing the muzzle's own *width* taper
(cheek-width to nose-width, in profile/3-quarter view), a completely
different measurement that happens to land on a similar-looking number by
coincidence. Conflating a length ratio with a width-taper ratio because the
digits are similar would be a new version of the same mistake that produced
the bear and the rabbit. On a live animal, cheek fur flare and nose
cartilage will change the *visual* muzzle-to-head length ratio from this bone
number in ways I have no measurement for — that's an
[INFERRED-FROM-PHOTOGRAPHS] job against your reference photos, not a lookup.

Related, not directly quantified: a Bioone abstract (Zagrebelnyj & Puzacenko-
adjacent literature; geographic-variation skull paper) states plainly "the
Arctic fox is a much smaller animal than the red fox, with a relatively
shorter muzzle" **[secondary quote, qualitative only]** — directionally
useful, no ratio given.

### 3b. Skull size, geographic variation — [secondary quote, not primary-read]

**Frafjord, K. (1993). "Circumpolar size variation in the skull of the arctic
fox *Alopex lagopus*." *Polar Biology* 13:235–238.** Paywalled at Springer;
I could not get primary text. Via search-indexed fragments: n=278 skulls, 11
skull/mandible dimensions, from Fennoscandia, Siberia, Baffin Island,
Greenland, Jan Mayen and Svalbard. Eurasian foxes larger than North American;
skull size decreases with increasing latitude (proposed resource-productivity
effect). No specific mm values surfaced in what I could access. A separate,
larger, more recent study is referenced in search results — "Geographic
Variation of Tooth and Skull Sizes in the Arctic Fox," *Annales Zoologici
Fennici* 45(3) — n=328 specimens, 14 populations — but I could not open it
either. **[GAP]** for absolute CBL/zygomatic-breadth values outside the
Nanova & Prôa mainland number above (121.30mm CBL).

### 3c. Ear proportions — [GAP, mostly]

I could not find a published ear length/height measurement for *Vulpes
lagopus* in mm or cm anywhere — not in a species account, not in a
comparative table. What exists is qualitative ("small," "rounded," "thickly
furred") repeated across dozens of sources, and one indirect thermal-biology
data point:

**Klir, J.J. & Heath, J.E. (1992). "An infrared thermographic study of
surface temperature in relation to external thermal stress in three species
of foxes: the red fox (*Vulpes vulpes*), arctic fox (*Alopex lagopus*), and
kit fox (*Vulpes macrotis*)." *Physiological Zoology* 65:1011–1021.**
Confirmed as a real paper (verified title/journal/volume via search), but I
did not obtain primary text, only an abstract-level description: the paper
catalogs which body surfaces run hot under thermal stress in each species —
for red and kit fox this list includes "dorsal head," but for arctic fox it
does not (arctic fox list: face, nose, front of pinna, lower legs, paws).
That's consistent with a better-insulated, furrier arctic-fox skull relative
to red/kit fox, but it's not an ear-size number.

A specific figure I found in secondary summary — "ears are ~22% of
heat-dissipating body surface in arctic fox vs. ~33% in red fox" — could not
be verified against Klir & Heath's primary text and should be treated as
**unconfirmed** until someone reads the actual paper. I'm including it
because it's a plausible, specific, checkable claim rather than a vague
adjective, but flagging it clearly rather than passing it off as solid.

Comparator that *is* reasonably solid: red fox ear length is commonly given
as **7.7–12.5cm** (~3–5in) **[secondary quote, standard field-guide figure]**.
No arctic fox equivalent in absolute units was found. **This is a real,
notable gap** given the project's explicit history of getting ear geometry
wrong twice — I'd treat "ear height as a fraction of head height" as a
photograph-measurement task against the six reference photos, not a
literature lookup, until someone can access Audet et al. (2002) directly
(see §7) or a real morphometric paper with ear/pinna measurements.

### 3d. Eye diameter, inter-pupillary distance, rhinarium width — [GAP]

Found nothing quantitative for any of these three, for this species,
anywhere. Not in the papers above, not in any secondary source. This is a
clean, complete gap. If these numbers matter as much as the brief suggests,
the realistic path is direct measurement from a high-resolution reference
photograph with a known head-width scale reference, or veterinary
ophthalmology literature on *Vulpes* generally (I did not search that
specifically — canid comparative ophthalmology papers sometimes report

number is a current blocker).

### 3e. Jaw/chin/brow on the live animal vs. the bare skull

No measurement found either way. ART_DIRECTION.md §4d/§4e's diagnosis (that
"chunky chin/jaw/brow" is a soft-tissue blending-radius problem, not a
skeletal-proportion problem) is not something the skull literature above can
confirm or deny — Nanova & Prôa's crania are dry bone with no soft-tissue

## 4. Body mass and dimensions

Multiple sources, given side by side because they disagree more than you'd
expect for a well-studied species.

### 4a. Body mass — [MEASURED, population means, secondary quote for the table]

**Audet, A.M., Robbins, C.B. & Larivière, S. (2002). "*Alopex lagopus*."
*Mammalian Species* No. 713:1–10.** I could not obtain the PDF directly
(see §7) — the numbers below came through search-engine-quoted fragments of
the paper, so treat as **[secondary quote]** rather than a verified primary
read, though I have moderate confidence in them because they cross-check
well against a primary source I did read in full (next paragraph).

| Population | Male mass | Female mass |
|---|---|---|
| Churchill, Manitoba | 3.2 ± 0.4 kg | 3.0 ± 0.5 kg |
| Alaska | 4.3 ± 0.6 kg | 3.6 ± 0.6 kg |
| Norway | 3.6 ± 0.5 kg | 3.0 ± 0.6 kg |
| Sweden | 3.8 kg | 3.2 kg |

Males reported as 5–20% heavier than females generally.

**Cross-check, [MEASURED, primary source read in full]:** Goltsman et al.
(2005), *Journal of Zoology* 267:405–418, as quoted directly inside Nanova &
Prôa (2017), which I read myself: mainland winter body mass is **3.2–4.5kg
(males), 3.0–3.5kg (females)** — consistent with the table above. The same
paper reports Commander Islands foxes are substantially larger: **5–7kg
(males), 4.5–6kg (females)** on the islands, a real, sourced example of
island-population size divergence, in case a "robust" variant is ever wanted.

**Disagreement to flag:** Wikipedia's "Arctic fox" article (fetched directly,
citations not independently traced) gives male mass as "average 3.5kg,
**range 3.2–9.4kg**" and female "average 2.9kg, range 1.4–3.2kg." The 9.4kg
upper bound is far outside every other source I found (even the larger island
subspecies tops out around 7kg) and I suspect it's either a captive/obese
outlier or a transcription error somewhere upstream of Wikipedia. Prefer the
Audet et al./Goltsman et al. ranges above for a "normal wild adult" figure.

**Fat content, seasonal — [MEASURED, primary source read in full]**, Prestrud
(1982, thesis) via Prestrud (1991): whole-carcass fat content averages 22%
in Nov–Dec (Svalbard), dropping to 7% in May–June; winter range across 38
carcasses was 3–38%, with one outlier individual at >50%. Relevant if the
project ever wants a "lean spring" vs "fat winter" body-volume variant — this
is a real, quantified seasonal body-volume swing independent of coat.

### 4b. Head-body length, tail, shoulder height — three-way disagreement

| Measurement | Wikipedia [secondary] | Alaska State Museum specimen ASM#2 [MEASURED, single specimen] | ART_DIRECTION.md (client-photo-derived) [inferred-from-photographs, already binding on this project] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head-body length | M: 55cm avg (46–68cm range); F: 52cm avg (41–55cm range) | 30in = **76.2cm** | 0.55m = **55cm** |
| Tail length | "about 1/3 of body length" (~18cm if 55cm body) | 13in = **33.0cm** | 0.32m = **32cm**, separately stated as "~60% of head-body length" (§4b) |
| Tail : body ratio | ~0.33 | 0.43 | ~0.58–0.60 |
| Shoulder height | 25–30cm | not given | 0.28m = **28cm** |

Shoulder height is the one measurement all sources that give it agree on
(25–30cm), and it matches the project's current 0.28m exactly.

Head-body length: the project's 55cm sits exactly on Wikipedia's male
average and comfortably inside its range; the single Alaska museum specimen
at 76cm is notably larger than every other figure I found — possibly a large
male, possibly a measurement-methodology difference (nose-to-vent vs.
nose-to-base-of-tail-along-the-curve vs. stretched-pelt length), possibly
just one big fox. I don't have enough information to resolve which.

**Tail ratio is the one with the widest spread, and it matters most for your
work because ART_DIRECTION.md already treats 60% as binding and a prior
independent reviewer (REVIEW-2.md) explicitly checked it against the client's
real reference photos and did not contest it** ("I am **not** contesting the
tail length measurement (60%, to spec). My tail criticism is shape, volume
and carriage only."). That's a stronger evidentiary basis (checked against
photos of the actual intended animal, in winter coat, by a reviewer whose job
was to catch exactly this kind of error) than Wikipedia's generic "1/3" rule
of thumb, which reads like a rounded-off general statement that may not be
specific to arctic fox winter pelage or may be measuring a different
tail:body convention. I'd keep the project's 60% and treat Wikipedia's 33%
as the outlier here, but I'm showing both because they really do disagree by
nearly 2x and averaging them would produce a number nobody actually measured.

### 4c. Winter vs. summer mass

Both Underwood (1971) and Prestrud (1982) found **no significant seasonal
difference in body weight** despite large seasonal swings in food intake
(275% higher food consumption in summer, ad lib., per Underwood 1971) and in
fat content (22% → 7%, §4a). This seems counterintuitive (more food and more
fat in one season should show up as more mass) and Prestrud's 1991 review
spends a full page on why this might be a methodological artifact of
short-duration captive feeding studies rather than a real physiological
fact — worth knowing if the project ever wants to justify a summer/winter
body-volume difference: **the literature's best current answer is "no
measured seasonal mass difference in captive study animals," not "summer fox
is X% lighter."** [MEASURED — a null result, primary source read in full]

## 6. The "cute" question

The client's core complaint — "falls short of comfort" — maps onto a real,
named research literature (neoteny / baby-schema perception), and the
project's own reference-photo analysis (ART_DIRECTION.md §4c–§4f) already
independently arrived at most of the same features this literature predicts.
That convergence is worth stating plainly: the client's photo-based
corrections and the cross-species cuteness-perception literature agree, which
is reasonably strong evidence the anatomy team is being pointed at the right
features, even though the render hasn't hit them yet.

**Konrad Lorenz's *Kindchenschema* ("baby schema"), the foundational concept**
[qualitative, not a measurement, but the organizing framework]: infantile
features that trigger caretaking/positive perception across observers —
large head relative to body, big eyes, high/protruding forehead, chubby
cheeks, small nose and mouth, short thick extremities, plump body shape.
Multiple modern sources note these same features recur across juvenile
mammals generally (neoteny) and are part of why juvenile-looking adult
animals of many species read as "cute" to humans.

**Quantitative anchor — eye size, human faces (species caveat below):**
Frontiers in Psychology (2021), DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.674456, "Eye Size
Affects Cuteness in Different Facial Expressions and Ages." Method: real
photographs of adult and infant human faces, eyes digitally enlarged/reduced
±15% (calibrated around pupil centers; couldn't go further because at >15%
enlargement the eye's lateral edge started exceeding the face outline).
Result: large and unmanipulated eyes rated cuter than small eyes, with large
effect sizes (η²=0.48 adults, 0.43 infants, 0.25 combined study). **This is
human faces only** — I did not find an equivalent controlled study on animal
(let alone canid) faces — but it's useful as a calibrated sense of scale: a
mere 15% change in eye-to-face ratio is already near the edge of what looks
anatomically plausible on a face, and it's enough to move cuteness ratings
substantially. If your eye geometry is currently reading as "a dark speck"
(ART_DIRECTION.md's own diagnosis), the fix is unlikely to be subtle.

**Cross-species transfer of the same perceptual mechanism:** Golle, J.,
Lisibach, S., Mast, F.W. & Lobmaier, J.S. (2013). "Sweet Puppies and Cute
Babies: Perceptual Adaptation to Babyfacedness Transfers across Species."
*PLOS ONE* 8(3):e58248. Found that perceptual adaptation to cute/less-cute
puppy faces shifted subsequent cuteness ratings of human infant faces (and
vice versa), evidence that human and non-human juvenile cuteness share a
common visual coding mechanism rather than being judged by unrelated
criteria. Supports applying baby-schema logic to a fox face at all.

**Developmental peak, dogs specifically (context, not directly transferable
to an adult fox):** Chersini, N., Hall, N.J. & Wynne, C.D.L. (2018). "Dog
pups' attractiveness to humans peaks at weaning age." *Anthrozoös*
31(3):309–318. Puppies were rated cutest between roughly 6.3 and 8.3 weeks
depending on breed — coinciding with natural weaning age, i.e., cuteness
peaks exactly when the animal most needs continued care. This is a
*developmental timing* finding, not a proportions table, and your subject is
an adult, so it doesn't hand you a ratio — but it reinforces that the
mechanism is about retained/exhibited juvenile proportions, which for an
**adult** arctic fox means: how neotenized is an adult arctic fox relative to
a "baseline adult canid"?

**The real, measurable comparator: arctic fox vs. red fox as two adult canids
at different points on the same neoteny-adjacent axis.** This is the most
directly useful framing I found, because every number in it is sourced
elsewhere in this document or independently confirmed, and it's a real
biological contrast rather than an invented target:

| Trait | Arctic fox (adult) | Red fox (adult) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Body mass | 3.0–4.5 kg (mainland) | ~4–8.7 kg (typically larger) | §4a this doc; general red fox literature |
| Muzzle | "Relatively shorter muzzle" than red fox, on a smaller skull | Longer, narrower, more pointed | §3a; Bioone secondary quote |
| Ears | Small, rounded, heavily furred; possibly ~22% of heat-dissipating surface (unconfirmed, §3c) | 7.7–12.5cm, larger, more heat-dissipating (~33%, unconfirmed) | §3c |
| Legs | Relatively short | Longer, more cursorial | Prestrud 1991, Macpherson 1969 (qualitative) |
| Body shape, winter | Short, rounded, coat obscures the waist | Leaner, more visible body shape even in winter coat | Prestrud 1991 (qualitative) |

Every one of these is a real, documented, cold-adaptation (Allen's/Bergmann's
rule) difference, not a claim about neoteny per se — but the *visual result*
of cold adaptation in this species (bigger relative head, shorter muzzle,
smaller ears, shorter legs, rounder body) happens to be almost exactly the
Kindchenschema feature list. In other words: **the arctic fox is not a red
fox that a stylization pass made cuter — it is measurably closer to the
neotenized end of the canid spectrum than a red fox is, for unrelated
thermal-adaptation reasons, and that's why it photographs as "cute" without
anyone stylizing it.** The practical implication for the project: don't
soften the anatomy to make it read as cute. Get the actual cold-adaptation
proportions right (short muzzle relative to a *rounder, bigger-looking* head,
small ears, short legs hidden in belly fur, no visible waist) and the cute
reading should follow from correct anatomy, not from a separate stylization
pass — which is also exactly what ART_DIRECTION.md §4d already concluded
("cute here is not a stylisation — it is what the anatomy actually looks
like").
